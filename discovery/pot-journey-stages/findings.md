# Pot journey stages: findings

**Kind of Hop: a survey, with a short exploration of how the findings bear on the product.** It surveys how pots are commonly followed from clay to finished piece (published pottery guides, glaze-combination write-ups, existing pottery-logging apps) and sets that against what the project has said about itself. It writes findings only: no application code and no design decisions. Where the findings suggest something, they say "candidate" or "likely", never "the app will".

## 1. The author's pot journey: stages, failures, end states

### The main path

Published guides name between seven and eight clay states, from raw or powdered clay through slip, plastic (workable), leather hard, bone dry, bisqueware and glazeware. The first three are about the clay as a material; for a potter who starts from a lump of bought clay, the path of a pot starts at the plastic stage. The path of a single pot, in the order most guides give, is below. Names in the first column are the common ones; the author may use others.

| # | Stage (common name) | What is happening | Worth noting here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lump / wedged clay | A weighed piece of clay is prepared. (For slip-cast work there is no lump: liquid slip is poured into a mould, recalled.) | Which clay body (brand and type; a lot number if there is one); weight of clay. |
| 2 | Formed (plastic) | The pot is built (coil, slab, pinch), thrown or cast while the clay is soft. The author mostly hand-builds, sometimes throws or slip-casts. | Date; form (mug, bowl, vase); method (the author uses three, so the method differs from pot to pot); intent (one of a set, a test, a gift). A photo. |
| 3 | Leather hard (worked) | Clay has stiffened enough to hold shape and is trimmed, has handles or parts joined, is carved or decorated. | Trimming done; handles or parts attached and when; slip or underglaze decoration; stamps or marks. Guides split leather hard into early, middle and late; the middle is when most trimming and attaching is done. |
| 4 | Bone dry (greenware) | All the water has left; the pot is very fragile and ready for the first firing. | How it was dried (open air, covered, slowly); how long. Handles and fine details snap off easily here. |
| 5 | Bisque fired | First firing; the pot becomes bisqueware, which can take glaze. Done by studio staff for the author. | Date handed in and date back (the author may not know the firing date, cone or schedule, which staff set); result (survived, cracked, damaged). Cone if the studio says. Size after the firing if shrinkage matters: one guide says total shrinkage from wet to fired typically runs 8% to 14%. |
| 6 | Glazed | Glaze, or glazes in layers, applied to the bisque. | Which glazes, in what order, applied how, where on the pot. See section 4. |
| 7 | Glaze fired | Final firing in the kiln. Done by studio staff for the author. | Date handed in and date back; result; cone and atmosphere (oxidation or reduction) if the studio says; where in the kiln only if it is known. |
| 8 | Finished | Out of the kiln, checked, perhaps sanded or finished. | Finished photo; verdict (what worked, what to change); fate (kept, given, sold). |

Observations that bear on how a record could be structured (none settles it):

- **The order is mostly causal, not a choice.** Clay must be dry before its first firing, and glaze goes on before the glaze firing. What varies is detail around those fixed points (see section 2).
- **Trimming and decoration sit inside leather hard and are not usually named as separate material states.** Whether the author thinks of them as stages or as notes on a stage is not known.
- **Making method changes the early stages, not the later ones.** Hand-built work is assembled in parts (slabs, coils, joins) and the pot may have several joining moments while leather hard; thrown work has trimming; cast work is taken from a mould. From bone dry onward the path is the same for all three.
- **Stages are of two kinds.** Some are a material state the pot passes through by waiting (plastic, leather hard, bone dry). Others are something done to the pot that the author has to do (trimmed, handled, glazed) or that the kiln does (bisque fired, glaze fired). Lists of "stages" in the sources mix both.

### Waiting states and group events

- **A pot is often waiting**, not being worked: drying on a shelf, bone dry waiting for the bisque load, bisqued waiting for glazing, glazed waiting for the glaze load. These waits are where pots get forgotten, which is the problem a shelf walk exists to catch. Time-in-stage is therefore a candidate thing to show.
- **Firings are shared events, and for the author they are done by someone else.** The maintainer says bisque and glaze firing "is done by studio staff". The author hands pots in and gets them back, so the author's firing record is probably "handed in on, back on, result" rather than a kiln schedule. A kiln load holds many pots, probably from several potters (my reading of "large studio" and staff-run firings). One firing has a date, a cone and a result that applies to every pot in it. A record that stores firing facts once per firing and links pots to it would avoid retyping; a record that stores them per pot is simpler. This is a design choice left open.
- **Waiting on staff is a waiting state of its own**: handed in for bisque, handed in for glaze firing. The pot is out of the author's hands and the author's lever is to ask. Whether the author's pots sit in a studio queue, on the author's own shelf, or both is not known.
- **Bone-dry ware may touch in a bisque load** unless underglaze is applied (one guide says so), so a pot's place in a load can matter for how it came out.

### Failures and abandonment

A failure is not a stage of its own: it is a pot leaving the main path at a stage, for a cause. Published sources name these points of failure:

| Where it happens | What happens | Source's stated cause |
|---|---|---|
| Wet to bone dry | S-shaped cracks radiating from the base of thrown pots; handles and attachments pulling away. | Uneven drying between base and walls; one source also says insufficient compression is often blamed but is not the real culprit. Uniform wall thickness, even drying and consistent clay are the usual remedies. |
| Bone dry (handling) | Handles and fine detail snap off. | Fragility at this stage. |
| Bisque or glaze firing and cooling | "Dunting" cracks. | Temperature shock: parts of the pot expanding or contracting at different rates. |
| Either firing | Cracks that were already in the greenware show up only now. | Drying cracks can first become apparent in the bisque or glaze fire. |
| Glaze application | Dried glaze cracks or flakes before firing. | Glaze too thick (especially in layers), too slow to dry on a thin or damp pot, or rough handling. |
| Glaze firing | Crawling (glaze draws back into islands leaving bare clay), pinholes and pitting, glaze running onto the kiln shelf. | Weak glaze-to-body bond, gas escaping after the glaze seals, incomplete carbon burnout, glazes that run together as a combination. Layered glazes can behave as a lower-melting mixture than either alone, so a pair that is stable alone can run in combination; the source says to fire such work on a "cookie" to protect the shelf. |

Points worth noting for a record:

- **The stage where a failure was *noticed* is often later than the stage where it was *caused*.** A drying crack seen at the bisque unload was caused during drying. A record that holds both "where it broke" and "where I think it started" keeps the lesson.
- **A pot that fails is a source of learning, not just a loss.** The assumption in the project's brief (failed pots are kept as records with an end state) fits this: what is worth keeping is the stage, the suspected cause, a photo, and what to change.
- **The author wants a photo of the failure.** The maintainer's words: "I also want to see a photo of the failure". The record of a failed pot therefore holds a photo as well as the stage and a note. Whether the author also wants a suspected cause, or a note on what to change, was not said; that remains a candidate from the sources.
- **A cracked pot is not always an ended pot.** Whether the author keeps, repairs, reclaims or bins failed pots is not known; clay that is still unfired can usually be reclaimed into a lump again (recalled), which makes a lump the start of the next pot and the end of this one.

### End states

Candidate end states, from the stages and failure points above:

- **Finished**: glaze fired and accepted.
- **Finished, with a flaw**: kept or used as a "second" (the word is common among potters; recalled).
- **Failed at a stage** (cracked, broke, glaze fault), with the stage and a suspected cause.
- **Reclaimed**: unfired clay returned to the lump (recalled).
- **Abandoned**: stopped before completion without a failure, for example left on the shelf or never glazed. The author's own stages decide how this differs from "failed".
- **Afterlife of a finished pot** (kept, gifted, sold, broken later): none of the sources gives it as part of a pot's journey; whether the author wants it is not known.

### What is worth recording at each stage

A cross-cutting view of the same table, ordered by how cheap it is to give and how much it pays back later. This is evidence for a later choice, not a field list.

- **Almost free, almost always wanted:** the date, a photo (stated by the maintainer for failures, and for the finished work the large studio may want to see), which stage the pot is at, and a one-line note.
- **Cheap and useful across pots:** clay body; form; weight or size; which firing it went into; cone.
- **Worth a free-text note, since it is what the next pot needs:** what went wrong or right, and why the author thinks so; how it was dried; what the glaze did on that clay.
- **Costly and probably only sometimes recorded:** measured shrinkage, exact wall thickness, kiln position, glaze specific gravity. Printed pottery journals include sections for them (the clay body, forming details, drying observations, glaze layering sequences, firing schedule, and a results section comparing prediction against outcome), which shows what some potters want but also how much there is to fill in.
- **Existing logging apps** advertise tracking a pot from the wheel to the kiln, logging firings, and organising clay bodies and glazes (Dasti), and recording clay type, shrinkage, forming method, pattern, texture, colour, decoration, and notes on handles, spouts, glazes and firing temperatures (Pottery Log). Only store listings were read; the apps themselves were not used.

The points below are the axes along which potters commonly differ. With friends working much like the author, they are not expected to differ widely, so each axis is a place a friend's habits could diverge in detail, not a known difference. Claims here come from general pottery practice (recalled) unless a source is named.

| Axis | How it can differ | What it does to the stage map |
|---|---|---|
| Names | "Greenware" and "bone dry"; "bisque", "biscuit"; "glaze fire", "glost", "final firing". | Same stage, different word: renaming alone must be cheap. The sources themselves disagree on how many stages there are (seven or eight) and where the list starts (raw clay, slip, plastic). |
| How a pot is made | Wheel, hand-building (coil, slab, pinch), slip casting, mould pressing. The author mostly hand-builds, sometimes throws or slip-casts. | Hand-built work is assembled in parts; slip-cast work starts from liquid clay, not a lump; "wedged lump" may not be the first stage. |
| Number and kind of firings | Bisque then glaze (the author's case, done by studio staff); single firing (glaze raw clay); a third firing for lustre, decals or overglaze; raku, soda, wood or pit firings. | Added stages | Trimming, joining handles, carving, slip or underglaze decoration, waxing the foot, sanding, test tiles, repairs. | Some treat these as stages, some as notes. |

Because the author's firings are staff-run, the "standing at the kiln" case is more likely "what did I hand in, and is it back yet?" than "what did I load?" (my reading).

**What is on a shelf is a mix of stages**, since pots drying, pots bisqued and pots glazed may sit in the same studio space. So the first question a walk answers is probably "what is at each stage right now?" Candidate groupings:

1. **By stage**: all pots that are leather hard; all bone dry; all bisqued waiting to be glazed; all glazed waiting to go into the kiln.
2. **By time in stage, oldest first**: a pot that has sat at one stage far longer than the others is the one a weekly walk exists to catch. Leather hard is the stage where timing matters most, because trimming and attaching need clay in the right firmness, and the clay keeps drying (recalled).
3. **By next action**: "ready to trim", "ready to load for bisque", "ready to glaze", "ready to unload". This depends on the author's own stages.
4. **By firing or kiln load**: at the kiln, the question is "what went into this load and what came out?". It is a group by firing, listing each pot with its result.
5. **By location**: which shelf or rack, where a studio has many shelves. Whether the author's pots are scattered over shelves or kept to one place is not known.
6. **By maker's intent**: sets, a commission or gift, tests. Only if the author wants them.
7. **By state of concern**: cracked or failed pots, to see patterns across them.

The hop that depends on this question ("Pot records and stages") also mentions a weekly shelf walk compared against the stages, so a walk is likely something the author does on a schedule; how long it takes, and whether it happens with the phone in hand, are not known. (Peradapt's reading of the maintainer's words, not confirmed: assumes a weekly habit you never described)

Things the sources suggest the view should make easy on a phone in a studio with poor signal (the signal and phone constraints are the author's, in Project Memory): finding a pot by a photo more than by a name, since pots of the same form look alike and potters seldom name them (Peradapt could not verify this: no source says this); a pot's stage visible at a glance; one-tap moves to the next stage.

## 4. How glazes and glaze combinations are described

Published practice describes a glaze combination as an **ordered list of layers**, each layer carrying a name, where it goes, how it is applied and how much, inside a firing context. The sources that show this:

- **Maker's catalogue of combinations (Mayco).** Each combination is two glazes named by code and name and joined with "over" (for example, one glaze over another), with the cone listed (06, 6 or 10), with the atmosphere and clay body noted (oxidation or reduction; white earthenware or white stoneware), and an application note: two brushed layers of the base glaze, then two of the top glaze, drying between coats. "Over" carries the order.
- **A ceramics author's recipes (Kline, in Ceramic Arts Network).** The three-glaze "Oasis" combination is written as numbered steps, each with a method and an area: dip the entire pot in the first glaze, dip the top third in the second, dip a third at the rim. Other combinations in the same book are written with "+" between names, which does not show order. Test pieces there were dipped for set times (two seconds for the first, one for the second, with drying between). Advice given: keep a second layer to the top third of the pot at first, keep a third or fourth to a quarter or to the rim, and use a lower specific gravity, shorter dips or brush and spray for later layers to avoid too thick a build-up that cracks and falls off.
- **A supplier's instructions (Sheffield Pottery).** Count the coats: two or three in total when layering two glazes, to avoid over-application; and the same glazes can be changed between brushing and dipping/pouring by thinning.

What these show, as the pieces one record needs to answer "which glaze was used":

1. **A glaze has a name that is not one thing.** Commercial glazes have a brand, a code and a name (code and name are used together, e.g. "SW-231 Lilac Speck"); home-mixed glazes have a recipe name (a potter's own or the book's); plain descriptions ("clear", "white") are used loosely. The same glaze may be written differently by two potters. For the large studio's possible interest in "which glaze was used", matching names across potters is the hard part, and it is the author's decision, not a data fact. (A shared vocabulary such as Glazy's recipe database, which holds recipes with ingredients, exists; whether it is useful here is not examined.)
2. **A combination is ordered.** "A over B" and "A + B" are not the same thing. The record must say which went first.
3. **Each layer has an area.** Entire pot, inside only, outside only, top third, rim, the foot wiped or waxed. A pot may have different glazes inside and outside, and different glazes on rim or handle, so "the glaze" of a pot can be several named layers in several zones.
4. **Each layer has an application method and an amount.** Dip (with a time), pour, brush (with a number of coats), spray; sometimes specific gravity; and whether it dried between layers.
5. **Surface treatments below the glaze take part.** Underglaze, slip, oxide washes and wax resist are layered with the glazes (recalled), and the sources' "layers" wording leaves room for them.
6. **The firing is part of the description.** Cone, atmosphere and clay body change the look; the Mayco listing fixes all three for each of its combinations. The same combination on another body or in another kiln can look different, so a finished photo is only evidence for its own context.
7. **Combinations get nicknames** ("Oasis" is the source's own name), which the author may do too. A nickname answers "what do you call it", the ordered layers answer "what is it".
8. **A result belongs to the record, not the glaze.** The finished photo plus a short verdict is the answer to "how did it come out". A stable combination is a thing the author may want to reuse across pots, which makes a combination something that can be named once and picked many times (a design choice, not a finding).

The assumption in the project brief (a pot's record holds the finished photo and glaze or combination together, so the glaze question is answered from one place) fits the above, as long as the glaze can be several ordered layers in several zones rather than a single field. How much of the detail the author wants to type at a glaze bench, after glazing, with a phone, is unknown.
